# Why volunteers stay (mini theory, fictional demo)

## Bottom line
- Volunteers leave when nobody would notice, not when the work is hard (the author's belief, V002).
- Being visibly needed does more than thanks or perks, and thanks only works when it costs the giver something (V005).
- Most of the evidence is borrowed from other kinds of groups. Nothing here is tested on volunteer groups yet.

## Definitions
- **Visibly needed:** a role only this person holds, so their absence shows within a week.
- **Retention:** still active twelve months after joining.
- **Efficacy vs. effectiveness:** does a practice work when done as intended, versus does a real, busy group actually keep doing it.

## Propositions

### P1 Visible need beats appreciation
- **Mechanism:** a named role makes absence costly to the group and visible to the member.
- **Evidence:** first-week owned tasks in open-source projects [M] (OS1); named jobs in fire brigades [W] (FB1).
- **Wrong if:** groups that give every member a named role lose people at the same rate as groups that do not.

### P2 Cheap thanks is noise
- **Mechanism:** recognition is a signal, and a signal that costs nothing carries no information.
- **Evidence:** handwritten season-end notes [W] (SC1). The rest is the author's view (V005).
- **Wrong if:** form-letter thanks and personal, specific thanks show the same effect on twelve-month retention.

### P3 Transitions are where groups bleed
- **Mechanism:** a member's first three months and a leader's exit are when the "promise nobody signed" (V001) gets renegotiated.
- **Evidence:** starter ladders [W] (CG1); founder succession [M in firms] (FS2).
- **Wrong if:** dropout is spread evenly across a volunteer's tenure.

## Decision rule: The Empty-Chair Test
For each volunteer, ask in order, and stop at the first "no":
1. If they stopped coming, would anyone notice within a week?
2. Is there one thing only they do, and does the group know it?
3. When did someone last thank them for something specific, in a way that took effort?
A "no" names the practice to try first.

## Predictions
| # | Prediction (with a date) | Tests | How we'd know |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | By end of 2027, at least one volunteer platform will report higher one-year retention for members given a named role in their first month | P1 | Published platform data or a study |
| 2 | By end of 2027, a study will find no retention gain from automated thank-you emails | P2 | Peer-reviewed or preregistered study |
| 3 | By mid-2028, groups that run an overlap year for a departing founder will keep more members through the handover than groups that do not | P3 | Case series across 10+ groups |
| 4 | By end of 2028, dropout curves from at least two volunteer datasets will peak in the first three months | P3 | Shared datasets |
| 5 | By end of 2029, a field test of the Empty-Chair Test will show a measurable drop in quiet exits | P1, P2 | A pilot with a comparison group |

## Scoring
Each practice in the coverage grid gets a score on two axes, efficacy and effectiveness, as High,
Medium or Low. These scores are judgment calls by the author and the agent, not measurements.
Another scorer could move several of them.

## Where the evidence is thin
1. No study here is about volunteer groups directly; every proposition borrows from firms, open-source projects or sports clubs.
2. P2 rests mostly on the author's own belief (V005) and one weak source.
3. The founder-succession evidence comes from paid firms, where the successor bench is deeper.
