# A founder stepping down is like a company's founder leaving

The author's words, kept verbatim (V003): "A founder stepping down is like a company's founder
leaving: the club has to find out it was never one person."

## What the other world does
- **Split the role before the exit.** Founder-led firms that plan a handover list everything the founder does, then give each part a named owner a year ahead. [W] (FS1)
- **The overlap year.** The successor runs the work while the founder stays on as an adviser with no veto. [M] (FS2)
- **A public handover moment.** A dated event where the authority visibly changes hands, so members know whom to ask. [A] (FS1)

## Translation
- Before the founder of a volunteer group leaves, write down the dozen invisible jobs they do and give each one a name (fills S4×NEED).
- Run one season with the successor leading and the founder in the room but not deciding (fills S4×GROW).
- Hold a short handover evening. Keep it about the group, not the founder.

## Where the analogy breaks
A company can pay a successor and promote from a deep bench; a volunteer group usually cannot do
either, so the successor may be the only person willing. Founders of volunteer groups are often
also the social center, and friends who joined for them can leave with them, which a company
rarely sees at the same scale. Nobody in a volunteer group signed a contract, so "succession
planning" can feel like pushing someone out. The overlap year can turn into the founder never
leaving. These breaks are why S4×BEL and S4×REC stay empty.

## Evidence strength
- Splitting the role: [W]. One fictional case series; reasoned borrowing, not tested in volunteer groups.
- Overlap year: [M] in firms (FS2); untested in volunteer groups.
- Handover event: [A]. Doctrine and anecdote only.
- Overall: reasoned borrowing. No study tests founder succession in volunteer groups.
