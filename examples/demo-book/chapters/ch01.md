# The ladder I bought twice

I found the second ladder while looking for the first. [S004] Same brand. Same color. I stood in the garage and laughed, and then I did the math on what I'd spent. [author]

I've climbed each of those ladders once. [S003] That's not a confession about ladders. It's a description of my garage, and probably yours.

## What the shelf is for

Here's what our library learned in its first year. It made 1,140 loans from 212 tools. [S002] The drill went out 96 times. [S002] The tile saw went out 4 times. [S002] Both numbers are the argument. The drill proves people need tools. The tile saw proves they don't need to own them.

When we asked members, 31 of 40 said they'd bought a tool in the past five years that they used fewer than three times. [S001] I was one of them. Twice.

A tool on a shelf isn't an asset. It's a bet that the job will come back before the tool rusts. [opinion] Most of those bets lose. [opinion]

## The strongest objection

The best case against me goes like this. You don't buy a ladder for the hours you climb it. You buy it so that when the gutter overflows in a storm, the ladder is there, tonight, without a phone call or a trip. Readiness is a real good, and it has a price. A library sells access on its schedule, not yours. For a household with kids, two jobs and no slack, the hour lost to a pickup costs more than the ladder ever did. Calling idle time waste misses what the idle time buys.

That's a fair hit. Readiness matters for the tools you can't wait for: the plunger, the flashlight, the fire extinguisher. [opinion] It matters a lot less for the tile saw. [opinion] The trick is sorting the readiness tools from the once-a-year tools, and most garages never do. [opinion]

## Where this leaves the thesis

Advanced, with a fence around it. Idle time is waste for the once-a-year tools and insurance for the emergency ones. [opinion] Chapter 3 tests the fence: what happens when the tool you need is checked out.
